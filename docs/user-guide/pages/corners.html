Corner Analyzer

On the comparison page, the Corner Analyzer goes through the track one segment at a time and puts lap A and lap B side by side.

Which segments

The analyzer uses the segments both laps share. Until the day is saved, these are the segments proposed from the best lap, and a note says so. If the two laps have no shared segments, the card says so and offers Use the theoretical best’s segments.

Picking a segment

Use the arrows (Previous segment, Next segment) or the menu, for example Corner 3 · corner · 142 m. Choosing a segment zooms all charts to it and puts the cursor in it.

Speed through the segment

The chart shows both laps' speed through the segment (shaded) and just around it. Markers along the bottom: ▲ braking point, ◆ throttle pickup, ● lowest speed, and a dashed line at the apex. Tap or drag sideways to move the cursor.

A missing value shows — with the reason, such as no brake channel or lap not fully covered here. Values worked out from deceleration instead of a pedal are marked inferred. The brake channel is used only when it shows the braking. When it has (almost) no data, or is not pressed in at least half of the hard brakings the longitudinal G shows (three or more are needed to judge), or its scale is not known (see below), the whole session's braking points and braking and coasting time are worked out from deceleration instead and marked inferred. This needs a longitudinal G recorded in g (or with no unit) that reaches at least 0.3 g of braking somewhere in the session; without one, the brake channel is kept, unless its scale is not known (see below). The brake chart and the Brake (measured) map layer in Compare still show the recorded brake. The corner details on the day page add the brake channel does not show the braking (or the brake channel's scale (0–1 or %) is not known), and the coach does not count it as a pedal. A brake recorded in another unit than % (a brake with no unit is judged as above) is not replaced unless it has (almost) no data: it shows channel unit not supported. A pedal with no unit whose scale nothing shows gives pedal scale (0–1 or %) not known (see G-G, driving states and coasting).

Zoom to segment zooms the charts again; Lap A here and Lap B here open a lap at the segment start.

Corner details from the day page

On the day page, tap a corner in the Where the time goes list for that lap's corner figures against the best lap:

  • Entry, minimum and exit speed; braking point before the corner; braking time; peak deceleration; throttle pickup.
  • Each figure says whether it came from the brake or throttle channel or was inferred.
  • Where the time came from: the lap's time through the corner's Entry (from the corner's start to where it is tightest), Middle (through its tightest part) and Exit (on to the corner's end), against the best lap, for example Middle 1.33 s · 1.59 s · −0.26 s. The split comes from the track's shape, not from either lap's driving, so both laps are timed over the same metres and the three add up to the corner's time. A corner with more than one tight part (a double apex or a complex), or one across the start/finish line, is not split, and the sheet says why. A lap with a gap in its recording anywhere in the corner is not timed through the parts, and when that is the best lap, the sheet says so. Braking before the corner and the straight after it are not part of the split; the Where the time goes list shows the straights.
  • Best of N laps: who had the highest minimum and exit speed, the latest braking point and the earliest pickup.
  • Corner type: what kind of corner it is (see below).
  • Open in the Corner Analyzer compares the lap with the best lap through this corner.

Later braking or an earlier pickup is not automatically faster. The parts show where on the corner the time went, not why.

Corner type

Near the bottom of the corner details, Corner type says what kind of corner it is, in three parts. The same short line is on the corner's row in Where the time goes. It is the same for every lap you pick: it describes the corner, not one lap.

PartClassesFrom
Braking · from this day's lapsHeavy braking: braking on at least half of the laps, and typically at least 40 km/h slower at the corner's lowest point than where braking starts. Braking: braking on at least half of the laps, less speed lost than that. Braking (heavy not known): braking on at least half of the laps, but the speed where braking starts or the lowest speed is not known on at least three of them, or the speed unit is not known (the note says which). Lift: no braking on most laps, and on those laps typically at least 5 % slower at the lowest point than at the corner's start. Flat: no braking and less speed lost than that. A lift or flat needs at least three laps without braking; with fewer the part says Not known: fewer than three laps without braking.The braking points of the day's laps here, from the brake pedal or inferred from deceleration (the note says which; the two are never mixed: the way most laps were measured counts), and the recorded speed. All braking up to the lowest point counts, so a segment holding two corners is not judged by its first braking only.

Fast corner: from 130 km/h.The lowest speed of each lap in the corner (a corner taken at one steady speed counts that speed). Speeds in different units are never mixed: the unit most laps were recorded in counts (on an even split, the unit of the first such lap in recording order), and the note says how many laps were left out. Speeds recorded without a unit (and none assumed in settings) are read as km/h and shown as km/h (assumed).
Shape · from the trackIn this order: Complex: three or more tight parts, or a corner that turns one way and then the other. Double apex: two separate tight parts. Decreasing radius: the second half turns at least 1.3 times as hard as the first, and the tightest part runs into the last fifth of the corner (it tightens to the end). Increasing radius: the first half turns at least 1.3 times as hard as the second, and the tightest part starts in the first fifth (it opens from the start). Late apex: the middle of the tightest part is at least 60 % of the way through the corner. It is measured over the part of the segment that turns, so where the segment's ends sit on the straights does not change it.
  • The limits are the same on every track, so a Heavy braking · Slow corner means the same wherever you drive.
  • "Typical" is the median of the day's laps, and at least three laps are needed. A part that cannot be told says why instead, for example Not known: fewer than three laps measured here or Not known: no brake or deceleration channel.
  • A segment that holds several corners (such as Corners 9–16 in a day saved by an earlier version, or corners you merged) is not divided into single corners: it is classed as a whole, usually as a Double apex or Complex. A segment across the start/finish line has its shape, but no braking or speed class, because each lap's two halves of it are on different passes.

The type describes the corner and how it was driven that day; it is not advice on how to drive it.

Braking technique

Under the corner type, Braking technique says how the corner is braked into: on the lap you picked and typically over the day's ranked laps. Each line shows This lap … and typical … (N laps).

LineWhat it is
Initial hitHow fast the deceleration builds, in g per second: up to 85 % of its peak, from the last moment it was below a quarter of the peak (at least 0.15 g), or from the lowest point of a dip of at least a tenth of the peak just before the rise, such as a lift between two pushes on the pedal. A coast or a light lift before you brake does not count. A steady coast between a quarter of the peak and 0.30 g that you reach from nothing (engine braking) cannot be told from the start of the braking, and stretches the hit, so it reads slower than it was.
Peak decelerationThe highest deceleration in the braking, in g, with how many seconds into the braking it comes on this lap and whether it typically falls in the first, middle or last third. Both are by time.
Trail braking · inferredHow long, and how many metres, braking goes on while the lateral G is at least 0.3 g. It is inferred: braking while the car is already cornering, read from the lateral G, as there is no steering channel.
ReleaseHow fast the deceleration falls off into the apex, in g per second: from 85 % of the peak, after it, back to a quarter of the peak (at least 0.15 g), or to the lowest point of a dip of at least a tenth of the peak right after the fall; the same steady coast limit holds.
Brake to throttleThe time from the end of braking to the throttle pickup. Shown only when the recording has a throttle channel. It is looked for until braking starts again (the deceleration passing 0.30 g, or the brake pedal pressed again) and for at most 4 s; otherwise it says braking again before the throttle or no throttle within 4 s of braking. A throttle slower than about 10 Hz (an OBD one, about 2 Hz) places the pickup only to its update interval, so the time is shown to 0.1 s as about 0.8 s.
Brake pedal: application and releaseHow fast the pedal itself is pressed and let off, in % per second. Only from a brake channel of about 10 Hz or faster; a slower one (an OBD pedal, about 2 Hz) says Not known: brake channel too slow (2.1 Hz). An OBD column of a RaceChrono VBO (a name ending in -obd) is never read for this, whatever rate it seems to have: RaceChrono writes it at the file's row rate from an update rate the file does not tell. The pedal still shows when braking happens, but the hit and release then come from the deceleration only.
  • The deceleration comes from the longitudinal G channel when the recording has one with data, otherwise from the change of the speed, smoothed over a 0.5 s window (the line under the title says which, and the channel's rate). RaceChrono's longacc-calc and latacc-calc are calculated by RaceChrono, most likely from the GPS speed and heading, and are smoothed, so the hit and release can read slower than the car really braked. Laps measured from G and from speed, or in different units, are never mixed.
  • Figures from speed and from a G channel are not comparable. From speed, a hit or release quicker than the 0.5 s smoothing can show reads at least, for example at least 0.74 g/s: the real one may be quicker.
  • Units are read as the recording declares them, also from a VBO's header. A channel without a unit is read as g (G channels), km/h (speed) or % (pedals), and the section says so, for example no unit recorded: read as km/h (or as mph when the speed setting for recordings without a unit says so; a unit the file declares is never overridden by that setting); a pedal recorded from 0 to 1 is read as 0 to 100 % and says so too. The distances (metres of braking and of trail braking) come from the speed whichever channel gives the deceleration, so a speed without a unit says distances read in km/h (or mph) under the trail braking line.
  • The rate of each channel is worked out from its values, not from the file's row rate: a channel RaceChrono draws with straight lines between slower updates shows its real rate (OBD channels about 2 Hz). Known limits: a rate from about 7 Hz up to 10 Hz can read as 10 Hz, and a good 10 Hz channel may now and then be refused as too slow. The rate is decided from the first 1,500 moving samples of the file (about 2.5 minutes at 10 Hz), so a channel that changes how it is recorded later in the file is not noticed.
  • Braking is found where the deceleration passes 0.30 g and runs while it is at least 0.15 g, from up to 200 m before the corner to its end. Shorter than 0.3 s is a bump, not braking. The hit and release need a peak of at least 0.40 g, at least three samples, and a channel of about 10 Hz or faster.
  • A typical that rests on fewer than three quarters of the braking laps says so under the line, for example From 5 of 16 braking laps; the others braked again before the throttle or coasted, or names the other reason. The laps that kept a value may not be the corner's usual ones: for brake to throttle they are the laps without a second braking and without a long coast, so the typical reads low. The day's line leaves out a corner whose figure rests on fewer than half of its braking laps, and says how many.
  • Typical is the median of at least three of the day's ranked laps that braked here. A line that cannot be told says why, for example Not known: braking peaks below 0.40 g, no lateral G channel or channel too slow (4.0 Hz). A corner across the start/finish line is measured too: its braking may start before the line.
  • A segment holding several corners (such as Corners 5–6) is measured on its first braking, and its brake to throttle stops where braking starts again for the next corner.

The figures describe how you braked that day. They are observations, not advice on how to brake.
